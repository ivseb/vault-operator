# Problem discovery

How to get from a rough sentence to a solution the user actually wants. Written to stand on its own: it names no step numbers and assumes only that an agent is talking to the person who has the problem.

The user came here because the thinking is hard. Every question that hands the thinking back to them is a question you should have answered yourself.

## The one rule that outranks every question below

**This is a conversation, not an intake form.** Everything in this file is a purpose you have to satisfy, never a script you work through. Three failures kill it, and all three are avoidable.

**1. The absurd question.** The wording is derived from what the user just said, or it is wrong.

> User: "Ich benötige ein Daily Briefing."
> Agent: "Was kostet dich das heute? Beschreib mir den letzten konkreten Fall."

There is no last case. Nothing exists. They asked for something they do NOT have, and you asked what it costs them to do it. One turn, and they know a form is running.

**2. Narrating your own machinery.**

> Agent: "Jetzt starte ich den Discovery-Dialog. Deine Anfrage ist ein Wunsch, es existiert noch kein Briefing, kein Format. Das ist der richtige Einstieg für Schritt 0b."

The user does not know what a discovery dialogue is, did not ask to be categorised, and has never heard of step 0b. Classify silently. Then ask like a colleague:

> "Briefing worüber? Erzähl mir, was heute früh drin gestanden hätte, wenn du es schon gehabt hättest."

**3. Chopping up their thinking.** Assume the user may be dictating. Speech rambles, loops back, corrects itself, and puts the important thing at the end. Give them room:

- **Openers, not slots.** "Erzähl mir vom letzten Freitag" invites a story and delivers five facts. "Wie viele Minuten dauert Schritt drei?" delivers one, and teaches them to answer in fragments.
- **Harvest before you ask.** A long spoken answer usually contains half the brief already, buried and out of order. Take everything that is in there first. Asking for something they already said is the fastest way to make them stop talking.
- **Never demand a format.** No "beantworte bitte a, b, c", no numbered questionnaires, no "liste mir auf". They talk; you structure.
- **After a long answer, reflect instead of interrogating.** Say the situation back in the right order and ask whether it fits. That gives them the correction and the thinking space at once, and it usually surfaces what they forgot.

No throat-clearing either. Not "Guter Ausgangspunkt", not "Damit ich verstehe, was du wirklich brauchst". Ask.

## Beat 1: the current reality, and it has three shapes

The purpose is always the same: **understand what is true today, what it costs, and what surrounds it.** The shape of the question depends on whether the thing the user wants already happens.

The rule that generalizes: **look for the as-is of the NEED, not of the artifact.** Even when the artifact does not exist, the need does, and the user is meeting it somehow today, badly or not at all. That is your as-is.

### Shape A: an existing task that hurts

"Mein Team verliert jede Woche Stunden mit dem Zusammenfassen von Status-Mails."

The task exists, so anchor on one real instance of it and let them retell it. What you need out of that: the steps as they really run, a number (how often, how long), and the step that breaks. You rarely need three questions for it. A person retelling last Friday hands you most of it unprompted, and the rest you ask for afterwards, one at a time, only if it did not come.

Phrase the anchor yourself, from their words. "Erzähl mir vom letzten Mal" is one way and not the only one, and a skill that opens with it every single time reads like an interview guide.

The as-is process is the first draft of the solution, and you heard it rather than invented it. The number is what lets you advise at all: two hours a week is worth a skill, ten minutes twice a year is not, and saying so plainly is often the most useful thing you can do in that session.

### Shape B: a wish for something that does not exist yet

"Ich benötige ein Daily Briefing." "Wir bräuchten sowas wie einen Wettbewerbs-Radar."

There is no task, no last case, no duration. Asking for a baseline is absurd. What exists is the NEED, and three things about it are unknown and decide everything: what is IN the thing, where its content comes from, and what the user does after reading it.

Start with the content, concretely, because an abstract wish stays abstract until it has to name a fact:

> "Briefing worüber? Erzähl mir, was heute früh drin gestanden hätte, wenn du es schon gehabt hättest."

That single question forces the content, exposes the sources, and is built from the user's own word. Then:

> "Wie kommst du heute an diese Dinge?" (the as-is of the need: scrolling through mail, asking colleagues, or not at all)
> "Was geht dir heute durch die Lappen, weil du das nicht hast?" (the cost of the ABSENCE, which is what replaces the duration question here)
> "Was machst du damit, wenn du es gelesen hast?" (the decision it enables. A briefing nobody acts on is a newsletter, and the skill for it is not worth building.)

Note what the cost question became. For shape A the cost is time spent. For shape B the cost is what slips through, and it is often much larger.

### What harvesting looks like

The user dictates. It comes out like this:

> "Also im Grunde, ich fang morgens um sieben an und das Erste was ich mache ist Mails durchgehen, das sind so vierzig, fünfzig Stück über Nacht, und da ist eigentlich fast nichts Wichtiges dabei, aber ich muss trotzdem alles anschauen weil ab und zu was drin ist wo ich sofort ran muss, letzte Woche hatte ich einen Fall, da hat ein Lieferant abgesagt und ich hab das erst mittags gesehen, also das war dann echt eng. Und dann die Projekte, da guck ich noch ins Board rein, aber ehrlich gesagt nicht jeden Tag, und dann geht der Tag los und um neun ist Jour fixe und da soll ich sagen was ansteht und dann steh ich manchmal da und weiß es nicht so richtig."

Do NOT now ask "wie lange dauert das Mails durchgehen?". They said it: from seven, before the nine o'clock meeting. Do not ask what the sources are. They said it: mail and the project board. What is actually in there:

- the need (know what matters before the nine o'clock jour fixe), the as-is (read forty mails by hand), the cost (a supplier cancellation found half a day late), the frequency (daily), the sources (mail, project board), the failure (standing in the meeting without an answer), and even the deadline (nine).

So the next turn is a reflection, not a question:

> "Also: bis neun musst du wissen, was heute wirklich ansteht, und dafür gehst du ab sieben vierzig Mails durch, in denen fast nie was Dringendes steht. Und wenn doch, wie bei der Absage, siehst du es zu spät. Ins Board schaffst du es nicht jeden Tag.
>
> Habe ich das richtig? Und wenn ja: was hättest du beim Lieferanten anders gemacht, wenn du es um sieben gewusst hättest?"

The second question is not a new topic. It goes deeper into the thing they already flagged as painful, and its answer tells you what the briefing is actually FOR.

### Shape C: a clear build request

"Bau mir einen Skill, der die Dateien in diesem Ordner nach Datum umbenennt."

The task exists and the solution is named. Do not run a discovery on it. Verify in one or two questions that the named solution addresses the real problem, then move on.

### And in every shape: the embedding

Almost no task in a company is an island. Weave these in where they fit, not as a checklist:

> "Wer liefert dir zu, und wer bekommt das Ergebnis?"
> "Durch welche Systeme läuft das? In welcher Form liegen die Daten vor: Dateien in einem Ordner, SharePoint, eine API, ein MCP-Connector?"
> "Wer macht das genauso wie du?"

The data form decides the implementation, and this skill does no data engineering: a source nobody can reach cannot be conjured. In Vault Operator the usual source IS the vault, reachable through read_document, search_files and semantic_search; a source outside it (a web page, an MCP server) is the agent's job, not a script's. The reach question decides whether the skill may hard-wire this user's folder structure.

And one question that shape B almost always needs, and shape A often:

> "Soll das von selbst laufen, oder startest du es, wenn du es brauchst?"

A daily briefing the user has to ask for every morning is a limitation to name out loud: Vault Operator has no scheduler. The skill is still worth building, but the user invokes it themselves; there is no routine to run it unattended. Say this in the dialogue, not after the build.

## Beats 2 to 4

### Beat 2: one how-might-we question

Reflect the problem back in one sentence, then put ONE how-might-we question up for correction, built from the user's own words.

For a shape-B wish the reflection is a definition rather than a problem statement, but it does the same job: it makes the vague thing concrete enough to be corrected.

> "Also: jeden Morgen vor acht, auf einer halben Seite, was sich seit gestern in deinen drei Projekten bewegt hat, wer auf dich wartet, und was heute entschieden werden muss. Quellen sind Outlook und das Projektboard. Richtig, oder fehlt etwas Wichtiges?"

A user who corrects that sentence has just told you the requirement.

> "Also: acht Mails in acht Formaten, freitags, zwei bis drei Stunden Handübertrag, und den fehlenden Zulieferer siehst du erst am Montag. Richtig?
>
> Als eine Frage: **Wie könnten wir dir freitags einen fertigen Statusüberblick auf den Tisch legen, obwohl acht Leute in acht Formaten liefern und zwei davon oft gar nicht?**
>
> Trifft das dein Problem, oder liegt es woanders?"

One candidate, not three. Choosing between three rephrasings of your own complaint is abstract work, and it is precisely the work the user came to be spared. The protection against anchoring happens in beat 3, where the alternatives are concrete and the user is good at choosing.

### Beat 3: two or three ways forward, one of them without a skill

This is the first real decision, and the form flips here: stop asking open questions and lay out numbered options. Nobody talks their way to a choice between three roads.

Use the decision block from `SKILL.md`: the question, two to four options, each with a one-line what plus a **Dafür** and a **Dagegen**, your recommendation first and marked as yours, and the user's own answer as a visible option rather than a footnote. Render it through an `AskUserQuestion` tool if the session has one, otherwise as a numbered list. An option without a Dagegen is a lie, and the user will find the downside later, without you.

Here of all places the own-answer option earns its keep. You have known their world for ten minutes. A user who says "eigentlich brauche ich das gar nicht wöchentlich, nur zum Quartalsende" has just saved the whole build, and they can only say it if the list leaves room for it.

This is one decision, and it is the only one this turn. Do not also ask which folder the files live in, or whether it should run on a schedule. Those follow from the road they pick, and asking them together produces three careless answers instead of one considered one.

Check every option against what the runtime can actually do before you offer it: an option that has to be withdrawn later costs more trust than one you never offered.

At least one option must not be a skill at all. A change to the process, a template agreed with the people upstream, a decision the user should simply make. If every road you offer ends in building something, the user cannot tell whether building is the right answer, and neither can you.

### Beat 4: the abort signal

> "Woran würdest du merken, dass du das nach zwei Wochen doch nicht mehr benutzt?"

The answer, inverted, is the first success criterion, and it is sharper than anything "was heißt fertig?" produces. "Wenn ich die Zahlen hinterher trotzdem alle nachprüfen muss" is not a complaint; it is a requirement that the output carry its sources. For a briefing the answer is usually "wenn zu viel drinsteht" or "wenn ich es trotzdem nachlesen muss", and both are requirements about ruthless selection, not about coverage.

Then commit: state the solution, the baseline (what it costs today, or what slips through today), the target, how it gets triggered, and what is explicitly not included. Ask for confirmation.

## The as-is check: do not automate a broken process

Mandatory before anything gets built. Automated waste is still waste, only faster, and a skill that freezes a process flaw makes the flaw permanent and invisible.

Take the most suspicious step of the walkthrough and ask why, up to three times, until the answer is a business reason:

> "Warum tippst du die Zahlen von Hand ab?" "Weil jeder sein eigenes Format schickt." "Warum schickt jeder sein eigenes Format?" "Weil wir nie eines vereinbart haben."

There it is: the real fix might be one agreement mail, not a parser. Three whys, not five; five reads like an interrogation in German and the user stops cooperating. If the chain ends in "ist halt so", that is not a reason, that is the signal to propose a method (see the catalog). If the chain surfaces a process flaw, name it and put "fix the process" into beat 3 as a road, possibly THE road.

## Moves worth knowing, and why they work

You do not need help phrasing a question. You need to know WHICH question is worth asking, and that is craft, not language. These are the moves that pay, each with the reason it pays. **Make the wording yourself, out of what the user just said.** A move recited word for word into a situation it does not fit is exactly how "Was kostet dich das heute?" landed on a Daily Briefing.

- **Anchor on one real case.** A person cannot specify a process, but anyone can retell last Friday. Ask about one concrete recent instance and the requirements come out attached to it, in the right order, with the exceptions included. This is the highest-yield move in the whole interview and the reason it never starts with "was brauchst du?".
- **The counterfactual, for anything that does not exist yet.** "Was hätte heute früh drin gestanden, wenn du es schon gehabt hättest?" A wish stays abstract until it has to name a fact. Do not ask them to specify a thing they have never seen; ask them to describe a morning they have lived.
- **Turn the cost question around when there is no task.** For an existing chore the cost is time. For something missing it is what slips through, and that is usually the bigger number and the one nobody has counted.
- **Three whys, until the answer is a business reason.** Not five; five reads like an interrogation in German and the user shuts down. This is what stops you automating a broken process.
- **Ask what would make them stop using it.** The single sharpest question available, and no model reaches for it on its own. Inverted, the answer is the first success criterion, and it is always harder than anything "was heißt fertig?" produces. "Wenn ich die Zahlen hinterher trotzdem nachprüfen muss" is not a complaint, it is a requirement that the output carry its sources.
- **Ask the receiver's view when there is a receiver.** "Was ist deiner Chefin daran am wichtigsten?" In a company the person asking is rarely the person judging, and the judge's answer outranks the asker's.
- **Follow the irritation.** The strongest requirement sits on the strongest annoyance, and the user has usually already flagged it in passing without noticing.

## The method catalog: triggers, methods, dosage

When answers go generic ("ist halt so"), when a claim has no evidence behind it, when the user guesses about other people's work, or when you catch yourself inventing: stop asking and propose the matching method. Do not keep asking the same question in different words.

The proposal, always in this shape: name the method, say in one sentence what it produces, say what it costs, offer to prepare it.

**In the session, agent and user together:**

| Trigger | Method |
|---|---|
| The user cannot describe their own process; answers stay abstract | **Do it together, here, now.** The user performs the task in the chat and narrates; you write down every decision. Observation is normally the most expensive discovery method, and it is the cheapest one available here, because you are in the room. The transcript is the first draft of the solution. Default recommendation. |
| Descriptions stay vague but real files exist | **Show me the artifact.** "Schick mir die letzten drei Statusmails und den Bericht, den du daraus gebaut hast." Real inputs beat any description, and they become the fixtures for the golden records later. |
| Several parties or departments are involved and it is unclear who delivers and who receives | **Stakeholder sketch.** List every actor with one line each: liefert was, bekommt was, entscheidet was. Five minutes in the chat. The most dangerous actor is the one with influence and no interest. |
| The pain spans several stations or systems | **Touchpoint walk.** Walk the path of the work piece through the systems it touches, one line per station: was passiert, was hakt. The dips are where to intervene. |
| Everything sounds too smooth; nobody has named a risk | **Pre-mortem.** "Es ist ein halbes Jahr später, niemand benutzt die Lösung. Warum?" Five minutes, and it surfaces the risk nobody wanted to name. |

**Prepared in the session, run by the user, results brought back:**

| Trigger | Method |
|---|---|
| The user guesses about colleagues' work instead of describing their own | **Two short colleague interviews.** You prepare five open questions, the user talks to two or three people who do the same task, and brings back what surprised them. Prepare the questions, do not just name the method. |
| The problem has regulatory or technical implications nobody in the chat can settle | **One question to an expert.** Pick the expert for ONE specific question, prepare that question, capture the answer verbatim. |
| The user orders a skill for work they have never done themselves | **Self-test.** The user does the task once, by hand, before anything gets automated. Log the friction points as they happen. |

**Dosage rules, and they are the difference between method and theater:**

- **No method without a trigger.** If the user's input is sufficient, keep going. Padding the session with methods teaches the user that the tool wastes their time.
- **The light one before the heavy one.** A five-minute artifact look before a colleague interview; an interview before a self-test.
- **Prepare, do not name-drop.** After proposing, build the artifact the method needs (the question list, the sketch skeleton), and say what to bring back. A method name without preparation is homework, and a user who leaves with homework does not come back.
- **One method per stall.** If the method did not unblock the question, decide with what you have and say what assumption you are making. A second method on the same gap is an interrogation.
- **You run the chat methods, the user runs the field methods.** Never claim to have interviewed anyone.

Anything that needs six people in a room, ten recruited interviewees, or a two-week diary study does not belong here.

## Anti-patterns in the dialogue itself

- **The absurd question.** Never ask something the user's own sentence already answers or already makes nonsensical. Asking a duration for a thing that has never happened is the canonical case, and it costs the whole conversation.
- **Narrating the machinery.** Step numbers, internal categories, the name of the phase, the artifacts of the build. None of it is the user's business. They asked for help, not for a tour of the workshop.
- **Throat-clearing.** "Guter Ausgangspunkt." "Damit ich verstehe, was du wirklich brauchst, bevor wir etwas bauen." The user did not come for a preamble. Ask the question.
- **The questionnaire.** Beats are purposes, not a sequence to be worked through. If the answer already contains the number, do not ask for the number.
- **Re-asking what they said.** Especially after a long, spoken answer. Nothing tells a person faster that nobody is listening, and nothing makes them stop talking faster.
- **Inventing the process.** Never write down an as-is step the user did not describe. Filling gaps from imagination is the signal to propose a method instead.
- **Consultancy vocabulary.** The user does not know what HMW, JTBD, persona or value proposition mean, and does not need to. Ask the question, keep the label to yourself.
- **A wall of questions.** One at a time, and only when a question is needed at all.

## What discovery hands over

Seven things, and each one lands somewhere specific:

- The **as-is walkthrough** (of the task, or of the need) becomes the inputs and the data flow.
- The **environment** (who delivers, who receives, which systems, which data form) becomes the feasibility layer, and decides between MCP flow, manual hand-over, and data-request artifact.
- The **how-might-we question**, or the definition sentence for a wish, becomes the intent and the first draft of the trigger description.
- The **cost** (time spent today, or what slips through today) becomes the reason the thing exists at all, and the yardstick for whether it worked.
- The **trigger mode** (the user asks for it, or a routine runs it) becomes a design decision in the brief, not an afterthought.
- The **abort signal** becomes the first success criterion.
- The **as-is check verdict** becomes, when it found a flaw, a road in beat 3, and sometimes the whole answer.

Do not ask for any of these a second time later. The user answered already.
